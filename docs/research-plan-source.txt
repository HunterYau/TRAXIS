Structural Tomography: Simulation, AI Architecture, and Testing Plan
Consolidated project decisions and intended experiments • November 3, 2026
Purpose: This document consolidates the student-authored research plan, science research logs, and explicitly discussed workflow for the computational and model-testing portions of the project. It deliberately excludes purchasing, vendors, and general equipment sourcing. “Planned” means the procedure or comparison was adopted for investigation; it does not mean it has been implemented or proven. Open technical choices are identified separately rather than presented as facts.
1. Core research objective and output
The project investigates whether a fixed piezoelectric-transducer (PZT) network can reconstruct hidden backside material loss in an aluminum plate while choosing its next actuator, receiver, and excitation-frequency measurement autonomously. The central comparison is reconstruction accuracy achieved per measurement, relative to random, predetermined, and exhaustive acquisition. The work also tests whether limited training on real measurements reduces the simulation-to-real discrepancy.
The principal output is a quantitative two-dimensional material-loss field, d(x,y), where each grid value estimates the depth of missing aluminum in millimeters. This is a 2.5D thickness reconstruction: for nominal 3.0 mm stock, remaining thickness is t(x,y) = 3.0 − d(x,y). The system is intended to estimate defect location, footprint, and severity—not merely classify a specimen as damaged or undamaged. Maps will be visualized as 2D heatmaps and 2.5D surface plots.
2. Confirmed end-to-end development sequence
The discussed project sequence is: (1) complete computational planning; (2) build an elastic-wave simulation; (3) verify the simulation, initially against an intact plate; (4) construct randomized defective virtual plates and their known defect maps; (5) generate and cache the simulated actuator–receiver–frequency waveforms; (6) implement candidate reconstruction networks; (7) pretrain and validate reconstruction models; (8) build and train random, fixed, and adaptive selection strategies; (9) compare reconstruction architectures and sensing strategies under matched conditions; (10) change isolated model properties such as decoder depth and map resolution; (11) repeat training and tuning until validation performance stabilizes; (12) move to physical experiments, including a small reserved calibration/post-training subset; and (13) evaluate final models on untouched physical plates and record errors, uncertainty, measurement efficiency, and visual outputs.
The reconstruction model and the measurement selector are separate functional components even when trained or refined as a combined system. During a virtual inspection, the selector chooses one permitted record, the simulated environment reveals that waveform, the reconstruction model updates the defect map and uncertainty estimate, and the selector makes another decision.
3. Wave-propagation simulation
3.1 Physical geometry represented in simulation
The working physical reference is a 6061-T6 aluminum plate approximately 500 × 500 × 3 mm. Simulated plate geometries will include an undamaged control and partial-thickness backside recesses, principally circular flat-bottom defects. The research plan specifies candidate defect diameters of 20, 30, and 40 mm and depths of 0.75, 1.50, and 2.00 mm, with location and size/depth combinations varied. These are intended experimental values, not claims that samples have already been manufactured.
The simulated plate is three-dimensional because guided elastic-wave propagation depends on thickness and plate boundaries. However, the learning target remains a 2.5D map of backside material loss over planar coordinates. The finite-element mesh and time step belong to the physics simulation and need not match the neural network’s output-grid size.
3.2 Forward model and simulation validity
The simulation is intended to model elastodynamic wave propagation after a transmitting PZT excites the plate, including the arriving signals at receiving positions, wave scattering by defects, and relevant edge reflections. Finite-element analysis (FEA) is the established modeling approach. COMSOL Multiphysics is explicitly considered in the student-authored plan, but the final solver, exact physics settings, mesh, and time increment have not been confirmed.
Before generating a large defective dataset, verify simulated intact-plate responses against an undamaged physical plate using wave speed, first-arrival timing, waveform shape/envelope, and reflections where measurable. Perform mesh and time-step convergence checks and benchmark the computational cost of a simulation. Only after the baseline wave physics is credible should large-scale synthetic-data generation proceed.
3.3 Synthetic defect and measurement variation
Generate many virtual specimens with independently varied defect location, diameter, and depth; include intact specimens. The accepted plan also anticipates controlled variation of material properties, excitation conditions, boundary/support conditions, sensor coupling, and signal noise to make training less dependent on idealized simulations. More complicated geometry or multiple defects may be investigated later, but the primary physical validation targets remain controlled backside material loss.
Each virtual specimen has exact simulated ground-truth geometry. Its corresponding material-loss raster is the label for supervised reconstruction training and numerical testing. The research log documents a common 512 × 512 reference raster for fair comparison of 128 × 128 and 256 × 256 predicted maps; the exact reference-raster implementation remains a planning detail, not evidence of achieved physical resolving power.
3.4 Precomputed sensor-response database
For each virtual plate, simulate the permitted transmitting PZT and excitation-frequency combinations and save the waveforms recorded at eligible receiving PZTs. Each cached record must be indexed by virtual-plate ID, transmitter, receiver, frequency, and relevant waveform sampling/simulation settings. The dataset also stores PZT geometry and the plate’s true defect map.
The virtual testing environment does not solve new physics every time the selector acts. It queries the precomputed waveform bank and reveals only the chosen record. All other unselected signals and the actual defect map remain inaccessible to the inference-time selector. This permits many sequential inspection episodes with consistent underlying physics, while preventing ground-truth leakage into decision making.
4. Data preparation, training splits, and partial observations
Separate training, validation, and held-out simulation testing by entire virtual plate/defect scenario, not by individual waveform. All frequency and sensor records from one specimen remain within the same split. This prevents the model from seeing related waveforms from an evaluation defect during training. Retain generation settings, random seeds, specimen identifiers, and train/validation/test assignments for reproducibility.
The reconstruction model must be able to operate on partial observations because inspection begins with far fewer records than the full bank. Train it on varied, randomly selected measurement subsets rather than only on complete scans. Each observed waveform is associated with transmitter and receiver identifiers/positions, excitation frequency, and a valid/observed mask so the model can distinguish missing measurements from genuine zero-valued signals.
The simulation dataset is expected to contain many examples (the current research plan says thousands), but no final dataset size or completed run count has been established. Numerical data generation, train/test partitioning, and model validation precede the physical adaptation phase.
5. Reconstruction methods selected for comparison
5.1 Model A: direct waveform-to-map CNN with attention
The first student-authored reconstruction pipeline is a composite neural model: time-domain PZT response waveforms → a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D CNN) that extracts temporal/wave features → attention-based fusion of the individual sensor measurements and their context → a two-dimensional CNN reconstruction decoder → a spatial material-loss map. The fusion stage must preserve or incorporate which transmitter and receiver were used, their coordinates, the excitation frequency, and which records are currently observed.
This architecture reconstructs from acquired waveform information without first requiring a classical delay-and-sum damage image. It is the direct-learning comparison against the second, imaging-assisted pipeline. The exact number of convolutional blocks, channel widths, attention formulation, normalization, and training loss have not yet been fixed.
5.2 Model B: delay-and-sum imaging followed by ResNet/U-Net refinement
The second student-authored pipeline begins with delay-and-sum (DAS), a mathematical wave-imaging technique that constructs an approximate/coarse localization map from sensor measurements. The coarse map is combined with PZT observation data and supplied to a U-Net-style neural reconstruction system with a ResNet-based encoder and a spatial decoder. The final network refines coarse evidence into a quantitative map of material loss and remaining plate thickness.
This is a deliberately different architecture from Model A: a physics/geometric imaging prior enters before neural reconstruction. Model B will be evaluated for whether that additional prior improves localization, depth estimation, and measurement efficiency relative to the direct waveform model.
5.3 Model C: delay-and-sum without neural refinement
The science research log also identifies DAS imaging alone as a third reconstruction baseline. It is not a trained neural network. Comparing DAS-only against DAS-plus-U-Net and direct waveform reconstruction helps isolate the value of machine-learning refinement instead of crediting the entire improvement to the neural pipeline.
5.4 Uncertainty estimation
Both neural reconstruction approaches are intended to produce uncertainty information alongside predicted maps. The research plan specifically proposes an ensemble of model predictions to obtain an uncertainty map. Confidence estimates must be checked against actual reconstruction errors rather than assumed to be calibrated. Uncertainty may also be used by the adaptive selector, but an uncertainty heatmap alone cannot establish which unmeasured transmitter–receiver–frequency record will be most informative.
6. Measurement-selection algorithms
6.1 Three main acquisition policies
Random policy: choose the next eligible actuator–receiver–frequency measurement randomly, excluding records already obtained. Repeat with multiple random seeds to characterize variation.
Fixed/preplanned policy: use a predetermined order independent of current defect predictions. The student-authored methodology describes starting with opposite-corner/diagonal PZT pairs and then opposing edge positions, with frequency options under the same permitted acquisition rules.
Adaptive policy: use the current predicted material-loss map, uncertainty map, and previously observed waveforms to decide which eligible transmitter, receiver, and frequency to measure next. The latest student-authored methodology proposes reinforcement learning (RL), with a reward favoring greater reconstruction accuracy obtained from fewer measurements. The policy’s exact network, state representation, reward equation, and learning schedule are not yet settled.
6.2 Intended closed-loop selection protocol
A virtual episode begins with a defined initial observation set. The reconstruction model produces a provisional map and uncertainty estimate. The selector considers available candidate actions and chooses a new, unobserved measurement. The environment reveals only that cached waveform; the model reconstructs again; the selector repeats until the predetermined record budget or stopping condition is reached. During training, the simulator’s known defect may be used to calculate rewards or labels. During validation/test or real inspection, the selector must never receive the ground-truth map.
The research notes also discuss uncertainty-guided heuristics and supervised candidate-value scoring as possible simpler comparators. These were explored as methods, not finalized replacements for the proposed RL selector. If implemented, report them explicitly as additional selector baselines, not as previously confirmed completed models.
7. Training, combinations, and architectural experiments
7.1 Reconstruction and selector training
The student-authored plan specifies an initial pretraining phase of approximately 100 epochs for each reconstruction model paired with a selection approach, followed by within-category comparisons and additional training/refinement of the selected combined system (also provisionally described as 100 epochs). These are planning schedules, not proof that 100 epochs will be optimal. Validation performance and convergence must determine whether additional iterations or adjusted schedules are justified.
Evaluate reconstruction models separately from selection policies before interpreting combined-model results. To isolate architectural differences, compare the same sensor observations under the same train/validation splits. To isolate measurement selection, compare selector policies using the same trained reconstruction model and the same acquisition budget. After these controlled comparisons, test preferred combinations; do not conflate improved architecture with improved acquisition decisions.
7.2 Output resolution ablation: 128 × 128 versus 256 × 256
Both neural architectures will be trained or configured to generate 128 × 128 and 256 × 256 material-loss maps. This is an explicit planned ablation (a controlled change to one component). Compare against the same underlying defect geometry and evaluation scale, not merely by visual appearance. Report quantitative thickness error, defect footprint overlap/localization, inference cost, and whether the higher-resolution decoder materially improves real accuracy.
7.3 Decoder depth and alternative network configurations
A further explicitly discussed experiment is modifying decoder architecture—for example, adding another decoder layer or upsampling/refinement stage—and repeating training and evaluation. Such variations should be compared against an unchanged baseline on the same simulated test cases and measurement budgets. Architectural alterations must be isolated where feasible, so a resolution increase, extra decoder layers, or a switch between Model A and Model B does not become indistinguishable from changing the selector or training data simultaneously.
Retain validation results, reconstruction images/maps, parameter counts, and computational costs for each configuration. Iterate while validation metrics continue improving or until they stabilize, then freeze the selected configurations before evaluating held-out simulation and physical tests. A final model cannot be declared superior solely because it contains more layers.
7.4 Other explicitly planned comparisons
Conduct comparisons of Model A versus Model B, with Model C as a mathematical baseline; random versus fixed versus adaptive measurement selection; small versus larger measurement budgets; simulation-only training versus post-training with limited physical data; and different defect sizes, depths, and locations. The influence of extra decoder depth and 128-versus-256 output resolution should be reported as separate comparisons.
8. Measurement budgets and fair comparison rules
The research plan proposes recording performance after 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, and 330 measurements and comparing them with a complete/full-scan reconstruction. The full scan is a dense-data model reference, NOT the defect ground truth; known geometric dimensions and material-loss maps provide truth. The 330-record total comes from an earlier 12-PZT, five-frequency scheme and must be recalculated if the later eight-PZT arrangement is used.
This configuration discrepancy is unresolved in the supplied documents: one materials section lists 12 PZTs and a ring-style layout, while the later assembly procedure describes eight PZTs at four corners and four edge midpoints. The permissible full-scan count also depends on whether transmitter–receiver pairs are directed or regarded as reciprocal and on the number of frequencies. Therefore, 330 and the associated budget series are preserved as previously proposed comparison points, not as a guaranteed count for the final hardware.
Compare algorithms with the same defect, same available sensor records, same reconstruction model when testing selectors, and identical measurement counts. Use a consistent initial state; average random policies across repeated seeds; evaluate across several defect conditions. Record reconstruction error after successive acquisitions to produce accuracy-versus-measurement curves rather than presenting only a final image.
An earlier success criterion is no more than 10% higher reconstruction error than the corresponding full-scan result while reducing measurements. This remains a proposed target, not an obtained outcome; a near-zero full-scan error would make a percentage-only comparison misleading, so absolute error must also be displayed.
9. Evaluation metrics, plots, and recorded outputs
9.1 Quantitative defect reconstruction
Compare predicted depth d_pred(x,y) against ground truth d_true(x,y) in millimeters. Planned error metrics include mean absolute thickness/depth error (MAE), root-mean-square error (RMSE), defect footprint Dice coefficient and intersection-over-union (IoU), centroid localization error, estimated diameter/footprint error, defect-depth error, false-positive damaged area, and material-loss error. Report whole-plate error alongside defect-region error because extensive intact regions could conceal poor predictions at the actual damaged area.
A defective-pixel threshold will be fixed before final evaluation to generate binary damage masks used for overlap and false-positive calculations. Handle intact-control maps explicitly. Where an estimate of uncertainty is provided, compare prediction confidence with observed errors rather than assuming that higher confidence means higher reliability.
9.2 Efficiency and visualizations
The planned figures include reconstruction error versus measurement count; adaptive versus random versus fixed sensing at equal budgets; comparison of direct CNN, DAS-plus-U-Net, and DAS-only reconstruction; 128 × 128 versus 256 × 256 output; before-versus-after physical adaptation; simulation-versus-real results; predicted-versus-ground-truth maps; and uncertainty heatmaps. Display representative 2D depth maps and 2.5D surfaces with shared units and scales.
Record the number of acquired PZT measurements and, for actual hardware tests, elapsed time, waveform-acquisition time, switching overhead, and model computation time. Fast virtual replay does not by itself demonstrate faster physical inspections. A live closed-loop physical trial is required to substantiate any claim of real autonomous acquisition-time savings.
10. Transition from simulation to independent physical validation
The current specimen plan contains six aluminum plates: one intact control and five known damaged specimens with backside partial-thickness recesses. Two of the five damaged plates are reserved for limited real-data adaptation or post-training, while the remaining three damaged plates are held out for final evaluation. The physical training subset is intended to bridge the simulation-to-real gap caused by coupling, sensor variation, environmental conditions, and imperfect wave modeling.
Measure and document actual defect positions, diameters/footprints, depths, and remaining thickness to supply physical ground truth. The final three damaged specimens may not be used for weight updates, hyperparameter selection, decoder choice, threshold setting, or sensor-policy refinement. Repeated scans of the same plate measure repeatability but do not increase the number of independent physical specimens.
Run the selected reconstruction models and sensing policies against unseen physical conditions, preserving equal-budget comparisons. At least one physical sequence must operate closed-loop: the model chooses a measurement, the system actually excites/records that waveform, and the next action depends on the updated reconstruction. Report results for each held-out plate individually as well as aggregate statistics, and distinguish offline waveform replay from live autonomous acquisition.
11. Implementation checkpoints and decisions not yet finalized
Checkpoint 1 — credible intact-plate wave simulation; validated wave arrival timing and basic waveform agreement; measured solver cost. Checkpoint 2 — reproducible dataset with known defect labels, intact cases, physical variations, and specimen-level separation. Checkpoint 3 — working Model A, Model B, and DAS-only baseline; 128/256 map outputs. Checkpoint 4 — measured random/fixed/adaptive equal-budget simulation results, with actual learning curves and uncertainty calibration. Checkpoint 5 — controlled architecture and decoder ablations, followed by a stable selected configuration. Checkpoint 6 — limited physical calibration and protected final holdout evaluation.
Still unresolved, and therefore not to be described as confirmed settings: final solver choice and numerical discretization; excitation frequency list and waveform sampling details; eight-versus-twelve PZT layout and resulting full acquisition size; exact network widths, decoder-layer variations, learning rate and loss weighting; RL state/action encoding and reward formula; precise confidence-calibration method; total synthetic specimen count; and the final stopping/acceptance rule. All such values are to be determined empirically without inspecting final held-out test outcomes.
